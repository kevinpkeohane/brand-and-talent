AI Readiness Guide for Commercial Leaders

AI readiness is a commercial question before it is a technology one. Start with the commercial constraint, then assess five areas: commercial priorities, decision rights and governance, knowledge quality, workflow fit, and measurement. Build the operating model before the tool stack, and expand from governed use cases rather than declaring the company AI-ready.

An AI readiness guide for leaders who need to turn scattered experiments into governed, useful systems that improve speed, decisions, and growth at scale.

A sales team is using a public chatbot to draft proposals. Marketing has bought three AI writing tools. Operations is testing copilots. IT is warning everyone not to paste customer data into anything. That is not an AI strategy. It is the usual pattern of fast adoption arriving before commercial ownership.

This AI readiness guide is for leaders who want to turn that activity into a system that improves growth, speed, and customer experience without creating another disconnected technology program. The question is not whether your company can access AI. It can. The question is whether the business has the clarity, data, governance, and operating discipline to make AI useful where the stakes are real.

AI readiness is a commercial question

Most readiness assessments start with technology. They inventory platforms, score data maturity, and produce a heat map. Those inputs matter, but they do not tell a CEO, CMO, or revenue leader where AI should change performance.

Start with the commercial constraint instead. Is the business losing opportunities because sales teams cannot explain a complex offer consistently? Is campaign production too slow to support market demand? Are customer-facing teams spending time searching for answers that already exist across decks, folders, and subject-matter experts? Is an inconsistent employee experience leaking into the customer experience?

AI can help in each case, but only if it is attached to a defined operating problem. A generic content assistant may make copy faster while making differentiation weaker. A sales copilot may surface useful material while spreading outdated claims. Faster output is not a business outcome if the source material, brand position, or approval process is broken.

Readiness, then, is the ability to make a sound choice about where AI belongs, build it around trusted knowledge, and measure whether it earns its place.

What an AI readiness guide should expose

A useful assessment does not ask whether the organization is "ready" in the abstract. Very few companies are uniformly ready. It identifies which use cases are ready now, which require foundational work, and which should wait.

That distinction prevents two expensive mistakes. The first is a large platform investment with no committed users or clear workflow. The second is uncontrolled experimentation that creates security risk, duplicate spending, and a trail of inconsistent assets.

A practical AI readiness guide should expose five things: commercial priorities, decision rights, knowledge quality, workflow fit, and measurement. These are connected. A strong model cannot compensate for weak source material. Good source material cannot create value if no one owns the workflow. And no workflow improves for long if leaders cannot see what has changed.

1. Commercial priorities

Choose opportunities where better speed, quality, or consistency affects a measurable outcome. For example, a professional services firm may need to reduce the time from client brief to credible proposal. A global product company may need to localize campaigns without losing message discipline. A revenue organization may need every seller to work from the same current narrative, proof points, and objection handling.

The best first use cases are usually frequent, costly, and bounded. They have a known user group, identifiable inputs, and a visible definition of a good result. They are not necessarily glamorous. A well-designed proposal workflow can create more value than an impressive chatbot with no place in the operating model.

2. Decision rights and governance

Governance is often framed as a brake. In practice, clear guardrails let teams move faster because they know what is permitted.

Define who can approve an AI use case, what information may be used, where outputs can be published, and who is accountable when the system is wrong. Distinguish between low-risk assistance, such as summarizing approved internal material, and higher-risk activity, such as generating customer advice, pricing guidance, or regulated communications.

This is not a legal exercise handed off to legal. Legal, security, technology, brand, and business owners all have a role, but one accountable lead must make decisions and resolve trade-offs. Committees can advise. They should not become a waiting room for action.

3. Knowledge quality

Most organizations do not have a data problem in the narrow sense. They have a knowledge problem. Their best information is fragmented across slides, shared drives, CRM notes, old campaign assets, intranets, and the heads of experienced people.

Before building an agent or retrieval system, assess the material it will rely on. Is it current? Is it approved? Does it reflect the actual positioning and offer structure? Can it be tagged by audience, market, product, stage, and ownership? Can the business identify the authoritative version when two documents conflict?

This work may feel less exciting than selecting a model. It is also where much of the value sits. An AI system trained on contradictory messaging scales contradiction. An assistant built on governed knowledge can give teams faster access to the company’s best thinking without asking them to hunt for it.

4. Workflow fit

Do not install AI beside the work and hope people remember to use it. Put it inside the moments where work already happens.

For marketing, that may mean an approved brief becomes a controlled system for producing channel variants, market adaptations, and sales follow-up materials. For sales, it may mean an opportunity record triggers account research, a first-draft pursuit narrative, and recommended proof points sourced from approved case material. For employee experience, it may mean managers can find accurate answers to recurring policy or culture questions without escalating every request.

Human review remains essential where judgment, risk, or originality matter. The point is not to remove people from the loop. It is to remove avoidable friction from their work and preserve their time for decisions that need experience.

5. Measurement

If success is defined as "adoption," expect a dashboard full of activity and little evidence of value. Measure what the workflow was intended to improve.

For a content system, that might be production time, approval cycles, asset reuse, and message consistency. For a sales system, it could be seller preparation time, proposal turnaround, win-rate movement, or the percentage of opportunities using approved messaging. For service operations, look at response quality, resolution time, escalation rates, and customer satisfaction.

Baseline the current state before launch. Then track both gains and failure modes. A reduction in drafting time is useful, but not if correction time rises because the system produces unreliable work. The right metric set makes that trade-off visible.

Build the operating model before the tool stack

Technology selection matters, but it should follow the use case and operating model. Buying enterprise licenses first often creates a reverse-engineering exercise: teams scramble to find problems worthy of the tool already purchased.

Instead, define the job to be done, map the workflow, identify approved knowledge sources, assign accountable owners, and agree on measures. Then determine whether an existing platform can support the work, whether a configured agent is needed, or whether a simpler process change will produce most of the benefit.

There is no prize for the most advanced architecture. Some high-value use cases need a carefully governed prompt library and clear review process. Others require retrieval, integrations, permissions, audit trails, and custom orchestration. It depends on the sensitivity of the work, the variability of inputs, and the cost of being wrong.

This is where many strategy firms stop at the recommendation and many technology providers skip the narrative. The commercial system needs both: a clear story that tells people what good looks like, and infrastructure that makes good work repeatable.

A 90-day path from experimentation to evidence

The first 90 days should produce evidence, not theater. Start by interviewing the leaders and frontline users closest to the targeted workflow. Review the actual inputs, outputs, approvals, and handoffs. Do not rely on process maps alone. The work people say they do and the work they actually do are often different.

Select one or two use cases with enough value to matter and enough boundaries to control. Build a minimum viable system around approved sources, named owners, clear escalation rules, and a defined human review point. Train users on judgment as well as prompts. They need to know when to trust an output, when to challenge it, and when not to use it.

Run the pilot against a baseline for several weeks. Listen for friction, but do not let preferences alone decide the outcome. If the system saves time, improves consistency, and fits the workflow, expand it. If it reveals weak source material or unclear ownership, fix that foundation before scaling.

The goal is not to declare the company AI-ready. It is to build the capability to become more ready with every governed use case. Companies that do this well will not be the ones with the loudest AI announcements. They will be the ones whose teams can turn trusted knowledge into better decisions and better customer outcomes at machine speed, without losing the human judgment that made the business valuable in the first place.

What to do next

  4. Expand only from governed use cases that save time, improve consistency, and fit the workflow
