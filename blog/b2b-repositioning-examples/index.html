7 B2B Repositioning Examples That Changed Growth

The best B2B repositioning examples are not cosmetic rebrands; they change the commercial frame: what problem the company owns, what category it competes in, and why a buyer should act now. The article examines seven: IBM, Adobe, ServiceNow, HubSpot, Accenture, Schneider Electric, and Slack. Repositioning can create pricing power and shorten sales conversations, but it is a high-stakes move.

A company can have a capable product, respected customers, and a full sales pipeline, yet still lose deals because buyers cannot quickly explain why it is different. The best B2B repositioning examples are not cosmetic rebrands. They change the commercial frame: what problem the company owns, what category it competes in, and why a buyer should act now rather than renew the incumbent.

That is a high-stakes move. Repositioning can create pricing power, shorten sales conversations, and open a larger market. It can also confuse existing customers if the new story gets ahead of the product, proof, or operating model. The distinction is execution. A credible repositioning joins narrative to the systems that carry it through the business: sales plays, offers, product language, campaigns, customer experience, and internal behavior.

What B2B repositioning examples reveal

Most companies reposition for one of three reasons. Their market has changed and their old category is shrinking. Their offer has expanded but their message still describes a narrow legacy service. Or they have become interchangeable and need to make a meaningful strategic choice about where they can win.

The examples below are useful because each company changed more than its language. It changed the evidence behind the promise, then gave the market a simpler way to understand the business. That is the standard to aim for.

1. IBM: from hardware company to hybrid-cloud partner

IBM spent decades closely associated with enterprise hardware and large-scale IT. As the technology market moved toward cloud, data, and AI, that legacy association became commercially restrictive. Buyers did not need another hardware story. They needed help modernizing complex environments without putting critical operations at risk.

IBM repositioned around hybrid cloud and AI, with Red Hat becoming a central proof point. The important move was not claiming to be a startup-style cloud provider. IBM leaned into its actual advantage: helping large organizations operate across old and new technology estates.

The lesson is precise. Do not reposition by borrowing a fashionable category if it denies your credibility. Reframe your accumulated experience as an advantage for the problem buyers have now. For IBM, enterprise complexity was no longer baggage. It was the reason to choose them.

2. Adobe: from creative software vendor to experience platform

Adobe was once understood primarily through boxed creative software. Its shift to subscriptions through Creative Cloud was significant, but its broader B2B repositioning went further. Adobe connected creative work, documents, data, content, and customer experience under a larger enterprise story.

That move mattered because senior buyers rarely buy tools for their own sake. A CMO, chief digital officer, or commerce leader buys faster content production, better journeys, and more relevant experiences. Adobe's positioning moved the conversation from individual applications to a system for managing the digital customer experience.

The trade-off is that a broader platform position is harder to substantiate. It requires product integration, clearer architecture, and a sales organization able to engage multiple stakeholders. A new headline without those conditions would have sounded inflated.

3. ServiceNow: from IT service management to workflow transformation

ServiceNow built its early reputation in IT service management. That was valuable, but limiting. ITSM is a recognizable category with an established buying center, yet it can make a company appear relevant only when something breaks.

ServiceNow expanded its position toward digital workflows and enterprise transformation. The story gave it permission to speak to HR, customer operations, risk, security, and business leaders, not just IT teams. Its platform proposition made the argument that fragmented work is an enterprise problem, and workflows are the operating answer.

This is a useful model for firms with a strong entry product. Keep the entry point because it provides proof and demand capture. But build a clear bridge from the original use case to the larger business outcome. Without that bridge, expansion reads like a disconnected product catalog.

4. HubSpot: from inbound marketing pioneer to customer platform

HubSpot became synonymous with inbound marketing. That association built a powerful brand, but it also risked placing the company in a narrow marketing-tools box as customer acquisition grew more complex.

Its repositioning toward a customer platform reflected a broader product footprint across marketing, sales, service, content, and operations. More importantly, it addressed a real buyer frustration: growing businesses were tired of stitching together software that created disconnected handoffs between teams.

The strategic choice was to preserve the distinctiveness of inbound while making it one part of a more complete growth system. That is often the right answer for a category creator. You do not discard the story that made you famous. You establish the next chapter as the logical consequence of that story.

5. Accenture: from technology consulting to transformation at scale

Accenture's evolution illustrates a challenge familiar to professional-services firms. Technical capability alone increasingly becomes interchangeable, especially when competitors can make similar claims about cloud, data, cybersecurity, and AI.

The firm has consistently positioned itself around large-scale transformation, combining strategy, technology, operations, and creative capabilities. Acquisitions in design and marketing services reinforced the claim that business transformation is not confined to systems implementation. It also requires changed experiences, changed ways of working, and adoption across the organization.

For consultancies, this is a reminder that a repositioning must be visible in how the firm sells and delivers. If a company claims to integrate strategy and execution but hands off strategy to one team and delivery to another, buyers will spot the gap. One accountable lead and a joined-up delivery model are not operational details. They are positioning proof.

6. Schneider Electric: from equipment supplier to sustainability partner

Industrial companies often face a hard transition from selling components to selling outcomes. Schneider Electric has moved beyond an equipment-led story toward energy management, automation, electrification, and sustainability.

The shift works because energy efficiency and decarbonization are board-level concerns with measurable financial and operational implications. Instead of treating sustainability as a separate corporate message, the company can connect it to resilience, cost management, regulatory pressure, and facility performance.

This example carries a warning. Outcome positioning needs credible measurement. If you sell efficiency, emissions reduction, or business resilience, buyers will expect baseline data, implementation expertise, and proof of impact. The farther you move from a product specification to a strategic outcome, the stronger your evidence must become.

7. Slack: from team messaging to the operating layer for work

Slack entered the market as a collaboration tool, often compared with chat products. That framing was easy to understand, but it reduced the business to a feature category. Its repositioning toward a productivity platform and a central place for work attempted to elevate the value from messages exchanged to work coordinated.

The commercial insight was sound. Knowledge workers do not want another destination for conversation. They want fewer fragmented decisions, faster access to information, and better coordination across people and systems. Integrations were essential to making that story believable.

For B2B leaders, the point is not to claim that your product is a platform because the word sounds larger. It is to identify the operating problem your product genuinely helps organize. A category shift must give buyers a better explanation of value, not simply a grander description of features.

How to turn a repositioning into commercial traction

A positioning statement is only the start. Before taking a new story to market, pressure-test it against five questions:

  • Can a buyer repeat the category and value in a single sentence?
  • Does the position make a real choice about who the company is not for?
  • Is there product, delivery, customer, or performance proof behind the claim?
  • Can sales use it to change a live deal conversation this quarter?
  • Do employees understand what must change in their own decisions and behavior?

If the answer to the fourth or fifth question is no, the work is still brand strategy in isolation. That may create a better presentation, but it will not reliably create growth.

The most effective repositioning programs build a message architecture, then translate it into offers, account narratives, sales enablement, campaign themes, executive communication, and content systems. They also establish measures before launch: win rate in the target segment, deal size, sales-cycle length, message adoption, qualified pipeline, and customer perception. Not every signal will move at once, and some shifts take time. But without measures, leaders cannot tell whether a new position is changing market behavior or merely attracting internal approval.

A repositioning earns its keep when it makes difficult commercial decisions easier. It tells product teams what to prioritize, sales teams what to lead with, marketers what to stop saying, and customers why the company matters now. Start there, and the new story has a chance to become the way the business actually operates.

What to do next

  1. Name the problem you want to own and the category you want to compete in
  2. Check that the new frame is something buyers can repeat and sales can prove
  3. Plan how the change reaches pricing, sales conversations, and the customer experience
  4. Weigh the risk of moving against the cost of staying where buyers cannot explain your difference
